# Engineering Philosophy

## Why I Became a Software Engineer

I did not begin my academic journey intending to become a software engineer.

My formal education is in Mechanical Engineering, where I developed an appreciation for mathematics, physics, and systematic problem solving. During my undergraduate studies, I also completed a diploma in Software Engineering, largely driven by curiosity rather than a well-defined career plan.

When I joined Tickets.com as a consultant, I was uncertain whether I could build a successful career in software engineering. That experience changed everything. Delivering production software, collaborating with experienced engineers, and eventually being encouraged to join the company as a full-time employee gave me the confidence that engineering is not defined by the degree you hold, but by your willingness to learn, solve problems, and continuously improve.

Since then, software engineering has become more than a profession—it has become the medium through which I explore complex systems and create tools that make difficult problems easier to solve.

---

# Engineering as Continuous Learning

Looking back, every significant career decision I have made was motivated by learning rather than familiarity.

At Williams-Sonoma, I learned disciplined software engineering practices.

At ViaPath, I learned how software evolves into a strategic business asset.

At Uber, I intentionally left a comfortable role to understand distributed systems and workflow orchestration.

At Walmart, I learned to navigate ambiguity and large-scale service migrations.

At eBay, I learned that governing change can be just as important as implementing features.

At Shrapnel, I had the opportunity to bring those lessons together while designing resilient platforms and engineering infrastructure.

Each role expanded my perspective rather than simply extending my résumé.

---

# I Enjoy Building Systems More Than Features

Features eventually become obsolete.

Systems endure.

Throughout my career, I have found myself increasingly drawn toward problems that improve how other engineers build software rather than implementing isolated business functionality.

This interest has led me toward:

- reusable frameworks
- workflow orchestration
- distributed systems
- observability
- platform engineering
- metadata-driven architectures
- developer tooling

I enjoy building software that enables other software to be built more effectively.

To me, that is one of the highest forms of engineering leverage.

---

# Repetition Is an Opportunity for Abstraction

One principle has consistently shaped how I approach engineering:

> If I solve the same engineering problem more than once, I should probably build a framework instead.

This philosophy is reflected throughout my open-source work.

Batch Orchestra grew from repeatedly implementing similar batch-processing workflows.

Workflow Scheduler explores reusable infrastructure for orchestrating distributed workflows.

My Embeddings library abstracts common patterns around local transformer inference and semantic search.

Rather than viewing these projects as independent experiments, I see them as opportunities to capture recurring engineering patterns and transform them into reusable building blocks.

---

# Simplicity Scales Better Than Cleverness

I believe good software should be easy to understand before it is clever.

Simple architectures are easier to maintain, easier to debug, and easier for new engineers to adopt.

While sophisticated algorithms certainly have their place, most engineering problems are better solved through clear abstractions, thoughtful interfaces, and predictable behavior than through unnecessary complexity.

Whenever possible, I prefer systems that are explicit, composable, and easy to reason about.

---

# Observability Is Part of the Architecture

Many engineering teams treat logging, metrics, and tracing as implementation details that can be added later.

I view them differently.

Observability is part of the system's architecture.

Reliable distributed systems cannot be built—or operated effectively—without understanding how they behave in production.

That perspective has influenced both my professional work and my open-source projects, where telemetry, monitoring, and diagnostics are considered first-class capabilities rather than optional enhancements.

---

# Reliability Is a Feature

Modern software rarely fails because individual algorithms are incorrect.

It fails because systems become difficult to operate, recover, or evolve.

For that reason, I place significant value on engineering practices that improve long-term reliability:

- clear service boundaries
- durable workflows
- idempotent operations
- fault tolerance
- incremental deployment
- recoverability
- operational visibility

A system that consistently behaves predictably under failure is often more valuable than one that simply performs well under ideal conditions.

---

# Technology Is a Tool, Not an Identity

I have worked with many languages, frameworks, and platforms throughout my career.

Each solved a different set of problems.

Rather than identifying myself with a particular technology stack, I try to understand the engineering principles that exist beneath individual tools.

Programming languages evolve.

Frameworks change.

Architectural principles tend to endure.

That perspective has allowed me to adapt across industries, organizations, and technology stacks throughout my career.

---

# Why I Prefer Go

Although I enjoy learning new technologies, Go has become my preferred language for building distributed systems.

Its simplicity, explicitness, excellent concurrency model, and rich standard library encourage writing software that is both understandable and maintainable.

I particularly appreciate that Go discourages unnecessary abstraction while making it straightforward to build reliable services, infrastructure, and developer tools.

For the kinds of engineering problems I enjoy solving, Go strikes an excellent balance between productivity, performance, and operational simplicity.

---

# Engineering Is a Team Sport

Software engineering is rarely an individual achievement.

The most successful projects I have worked on resulted from collaboration among engineers, product managers, designers, quality assurance teams, and operations specialists.

I value engineers who share knowledge, challenge ideas respectfully, review code thoughtfully, and prioritize collective success over individual recognition.

Strong engineering cultures are built on trust, curiosity, and continuous learning.

---

# Looking Forward

The kinds of problems that continue to excite me are those involving distributed systems, workflow orchestration, platform engineering, AI infrastructure, and developer productivity.

I enjoy designing systems that remain understandable as they grow, resilient as they evolve, and useful long after their initial implementation.

More than anything, I hope to continue working with engineers who are curious, thoughtful, and motivated by building software that solves meaningful problems.

Every project I undertake is another opportunity to learn, refine my thinking, and contribute to systems that enable others to do their best work.

---

# Guiding Principles

If I had to summarize my engineering philosophy in a few principles, they would be these:

- Learn continuously.
- Understand the problem before choosing the technology.
- Build systems, not just features.
- Abstract recurring patterns into reusable frameworks.
- Prefer simplicity over cleverness.
- Design for reliability from the beginning.
- Make systems observable.
- Optimize for long-term maintainability.
- Share knowledge generously.
- Leave every codebase better than you found it.
